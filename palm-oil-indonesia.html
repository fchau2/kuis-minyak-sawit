Kelapa sawitPalm oil in Indonesia: who wants it, where it grows, and what it changes

Indonesia grows more palm oil than anywhere else on earth. Work through this page in order — from why the world wants it, to where it is planted, to what it changes for the environment and for the people who live there.

Rainforest — irregular, layered, many speciesOil palm monoculture — one species, planted on a grid

01

Why the world wants itMengapa dunia menginginkannya

Palm oil is the cheapest and highest-yielding vegetable oil in the world. One hectare of oil palm produces four to ten times more oil than the same hectare of soy, sunflower or canola. That productivity is the entire reason it is in half of all packaged supermarket products.

In Indonesia

Minyak goreng

cooking oil (Minyak goreng) is palm oil — refined and bottled. Every home, food stall and frying cart runs on it. When the price spiked in 2022, queues formed and the government banned exports for a month.

Indonesian export

Indomie

Around 19 billion packs a year, sold in over 100 countries. The noodle block is fried in palm oil (minyak sawit) — which is why it keeps for a year without refrigeration. Indofood grows (menanam) the palm, presses (memeras) the oil at its mill (pabrik), and fries (menggoreng) the noodle.

Indonesian export · Mayora

Kopiko

The world's number one coffee candy (permen kopi), sold in over 100 countries. More than 50 million packs exported each year. Made by PT Mayora Indah in Tangerang, Banten. Palm oil is in the candy coating that gives it its smooth texture. Even carried to the International Space Station by NASA astronauts in 2017. Available in Melbourne.

Indonesian origin · Delfi Limited

SilverQueen

Indonesia's most iconic chocolate bar (coklat), made in Bandung since the 1950s. Holds 65% of Indonesia's chocolate bar market. Palm oil is used in the coating — without it, the bar would melt in Indonesia's tropical heat (panas tropis). Exported across Southeast Asia. Now Singapore-listed as Delfi Limited.

Where it all starts · asal muasalnya

Tandan buah segar · fresh fruit bunch

Every product above — the cooking oil, the noodles, the coffee candy, the chocolate — begins here. Fresh fruit bunches (tandan buah segar) grow on oil palm trees and weigh 10–40 kg each. The orange-red flesh (daging buah) is pressed at a mill (pabrik) to produce crude palm oil. The hard kernel (inti) at the centre is pressed separately to produce palm kernel oil, which goes mainly into soaps and cosmetics. A mature tree produces fruit year-round, which is why oil palm produces four to ten times more oil per hectare than soy or sunflower.

Around the world too

Chocolate, biscuits, ice cream, margarine, chips, pastries, soap, shampoo, toothpaste, detergent, lipstick. Biggest buyers: Nestlé, Unilever, Mondelez, PepsiCo, Procter & Gamble, Ferrero, Colgate-Palmolive. On a label it rarely says palm oil — look for vegetable oil, palmitate, stearic acid or sodium lauryl sulfate.

Also grown for fuel

Palm oil (Minyak sawit) is blended into diesel for Indonesian trucks, ships, trains and machinery. This is now the single largest use of Indonesian palm oil — bigger than any food company in the world.

When you read about palm oil, three different products come up. They all start from the same fruit but end up in very different places.

03

Where it growsDi mana ia ditanam

Oil palm (Kelapa sawit) was first planted commercially in Sumatra in 1911. As land ran out there, the industry moved to Kalimantan in the 1990s, and is now opening up Papua. Each island (pulau) contains multiple provinces (provinsi), and each has lost a different share of its tropical rainforest (hutan hujan tropis). The frontier has been moving steadily east for a century.

04

How the land is clearedBagaimana lahan dibuka

The same sequence repeats wherever the industry expands into new land. Follow the steps, then see where the chain splits into three different outcomes.

Step 1

Demand grows

food, soap, cooking oil (minyak goreng) and fuel programs all need more palm oil (minyak sawit) each year.

Step 2

Land granted

companies apply for concessions (izin usaha perkebunan). governments grant them — often over forest and customary land (tanah adat).

Step 3

Forest cleared, peat drained

trees are felled. canals are dug to drain waterlogged peat (lahan gambut) so oil palm roots won't rot.

Step 4

Land burned

Burning (Membakar) is the cheapest method to clear land. Fire reaches dry peat and burns underground — rain cannot stop it.

↓ splits into four outcomes ↓
Outcome A

Peat fire, air pollution and climate change

Underground peat smoulders for months. Haze (kabut asap) and air pollution (pencemaran udara) close schools and airports across Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore.

This drives global warming (pemanasan global) and climate change (perubahan iklim), making Indonesia one of the world's largest emitters of carbon emissions (emisi karbon) — alongside the United States and China.

Outcome B

Habitat destroyed

Forest that took thousands of years to grow is gone in weeks. Wildlife (Satwa liar) that cannot move fast enough do not survive.

Outcome C

Land taken

communities lose land their families have farmed or foraged for generations, often without being properly consulted.

Outcome D

Income, roads and services

16.2 million workers (pekerja) employed. Roads, schools and clinics reach remote districts. Smallholder families (Petani kecil) earn far more per hectare than from rubber or rice. Some argue this is sustainable development (pembangunan berkelanjutan) — others say it comes at too high a cost to deserve that name.

All four outcomes happen at the same time, from the same chain of events. That is what makes this issue genuinely difficult — it is not simply a bad industry, nor simply a good one.

05

Why the fires burnMengapa kebakaran terjadi

Peat (lahan gambut) is not soil. It is thousands of years of dead plant matter that never fully rotted because it sat underwater. Waterlogged peat cannot burn — and that is the key to everything that follows.

06

What is lostApa yang hilang

The animals at risk are completely different on each island — because an invisible boundary called the Wallace Line runs between Kalimantan and Sulawesi. West of it the animals are Asian. East of it they are Australian. As oil palm moves east across this line it stops threatening one set of animals and starts threatening an entirely different one.

Sumatra · fauna Asiatis
  • Orangutan sumatera
  • Harimau sumatera · tiger
  • Gajah sumatera · elephant
  • Badak sumatera · rhino

All four are critically endangered. Fewer than 50 Sumatran rhinos (badak sumatera) remain alive. The Leuser Ecosystem in Aceh is the only place on earth where all four still share the same forest.

Kalimantan · fauna Asiatis
  • Orangutan kalimantan
  • Bekantan · proboscis monkey
  • Beruang madu · sun bear
  • Enggang · hornbill

Around 100,000 Bornean orangutans (orangutan kalimantan) were lost between 1999 and 2015. The proboscis monkey (bekantan) lives nowhere else on earth. The hornbill (enggang) is sacred in Dayak culture and its image appears on Kalimantan's provincial emblem.

Papua · fauna Australis
  • Cenderawasih · bird of paradise
  • Kanguru pohon · tree kangaroo
  • Kasuari · cassowary
  • Kuskus · cuscus

Around 40 species of bird of paradise (cenderawasih) live here and almost nowhere else on earth. Papua has marsupials — like Australia — rather than the apes and big cats found in the west. The forest here has never been logged.

Garis Wallace the Wallace Line

An invisible boundary runs between Kalimantan and Sulawesi. West of it the animals are Asian: apes, tigers, elephants, rhinos. East of it they are Australian: marsupials, cassowaries, birds of paradise. As oil palm moves east across this line it stops threatening one ecosystem (ekosistem) and starts threatening a completely different — and much less studied — one. Conservation (pelestarian) efforts are very different on each side. The Leuser Ecosystem in Aceh is a protected area (kawasan lindung) and one of the most important sites for preservation (pelestarian) of critically endangered species on earth.

08

Region profilesProfil wilayah

Choose one region. Notice that in each case there are people who have gained from oil palm and people who have lost — and they often live in the same village.

Sumatra

Since 1911 · mature Smallholders and big companies
Environment · lingkungan

Most forest was cleared decades ago. The damage now shows up as consequences rather than new clearing.

Coastal peat (lahan gambut) is drained by canals, drying it out and releasing carbon. peat Peat fires (Kebakaran gambut) produce haze (kabut asap) most dry seasons (musim kemarau). Some land is now undergoing forest recovery (pemulihan hutan).

Stripped hillsides no longer hold rainfall. In November 2025, floods (banjir bandang) and landslides (tanah longsor) in Aceh, North and West Sumatra killed close to a thousand people.

Society · masyarakat

palm oil (Minyak sawit) is a normal livelihood here. Many families earn far more from it than they did from rubber (karet) or rice (padi). The tension between economic development and the living environment (lingkungan hidup) is felt most sharply in Sumatra, where both have existed side by side for over a century.

land is crowded, so disputes between villages, smallholders and companies have run for decades.

smallholders (Petani kecil) take on debt to replant ageing trees, and a 2025 government crackdown on illegal plantations hit them harder than it hit large firms.

Kalimantan

Since 1990s · peak clearing Mostly large companies
Environment · lingkungan

This is where most recent forest loss happened. Four of its five provinces accounted for 72% of Indonesia's oil palm deforestation between 2018 and 2022.

Deep peat swamp forest (hutan rawa gambut) was drained (pengeringan) and burned. Peat (gambut) here is metres thick and keeps releasing carbon (karbon) for decades once dried. Peat restoration (pemulihan lahan gambut) through rewetting (pembasahan kembali) is underway in parts of Kalimantan.

The Bornean orangutan is critically endangered, largely because its lowland forest was converted to plantations.

Society · masyarakat

Dayak communities (Masyarakat Dayak) held forest under customary law (hukum adat) that the state often did not formally recognise, so concessions were granted over the top of it.

Companies must legally share 20% of a plantation with local communities. This community share (plasma) is frequently promised and never delivered. Communities are also owed compensation (ganti rugi) for land taken — this too is rarely paid.

Roads, clinics and jobs did arrive with the plantations, which is why opinion inside villages is genuinely divided.

Papua

Since 2010s · new frontier Almost entirely large companies
Environment · lingkungan

Papua still holds around 28 million hectares of intact forest — the largest remaining in Indonesia. This is where expansion is heading now that western land has run out.

Oil palm clearing (pembukaan lahan) here doubled in 2025 to its highest level since 2018. Trees are being felled (menebang) at a rate not seen since the 1990s in Kalimantan.

Species found nowhere else on earth live in primary forest (hutan primer) that has never been logged. Preservation (pelestarian) and protection (perlindungan) of this forest is one of the most contested issues in Indonesian environmental policy.

Society · masyarakat

Indigenous Papuan clans such as the Marind and Awyu depend on the forest directly for food, water, medicine and ceremony.

Land has been rezoned for plantations — permits (izin) were issued without recognising customary land rights (hak ulayat). The Awyu took their case to Indonesia's Supreme Court (Mahkamah Agung), won a halt to expansion, but were still not granted land ownership (kepemilikan tanah).

Papua is already a militarised region (wilayah militer), so protest carries far higher personal risk than it does elsewhere in Indonesia.

09

Who owns it?Siapa yang memilikinya?

Indonesia's oil palm industry is not owned by one company or one government. The majority is grown by thousands of smaller companies and smallholder farmers. But a handful of very large companies control a significant share — and one of them makes Indomie.

The key point

Even the five largest companies combined only account for about 12% of all planted area. The other 88% belongs to thousands of smaller companies, cooperatives and smallholder farmers (petani kecil). This means there is no single company that could be boycotted or regulated to solve the problem. The industry is spread across the entire Indonesian economy.

11

What is being done?Apa yang sedang dilakukan?

No single action is enough on its own. The responses to palm oil's impacts come from governments, international trade rules, certification bodies and local communities — all at once, and often pulling in different directions.

Indonesia · sejak 2011

Moratorium on new concessions · moratorium

Since 2011 Indonesia has had a moratorium (moratorium) banning new permits on primary forest and peatland. Made permanent in 2019. This means no new concessions (izin) can legally be issued over intact forest — but enforcement is uneven, and existing concessions remain.

In 2025 President Prabowo's government reclaimed 2 million hectares of illegal plantation land. Critics note the crackdown hit smallholders harder than large companies.

Indonesia · wajib sejak 2020

ISPO certification · sertifikasi

The Indonesian Sustainable Palm Oil (ISPO) is Indonesia's own mandatory certification (sertifikasi) scheme, covering all plantations since 2020. It requires proof of legal land use, environmental management and labour standards.

Over 750 certificates issued, but the vast majority are for large companies. As of 2025 only 20 smallholder certificates had been issued — far short of the target. Cost and complexity are the main barriers for petani kecil.

International · voluntary

RSPO certification · sukarela

The Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO) is a voluntary international scheme that certifies palm oil as deforestation-free and produced without labour abuses. Major buyers like Nestlé and Unilever have committed to sourcing only RSPO-certified palm oil.

Indofood's plantation arm was expelled from RSPO in 2019 after failing to fix over 20 standard violations. Voluntary certification (sertifikasi sukarela) only works if companies take it seriously.

European Union · berlaku 2025–2026

EU Deforestation Regulation · regulasi deforestasi

The EUDR bans deforestation-linked palm oil, soy, cocoa, coffee and timber from entering the EU market. Companies must trace their supply chains to the plantation level and prove no deforestation after 31 December 2020.

Originally due 2024, delayed twice — now applying from December 2026 for large companies. Indonesia has lobbied hard for ISPO to be recognised as a compliance pathway. The EUDR is the biggest external pressure on Indonesia's palm oil industry right now.

Indonesia · 2016–2024

Peat restoration · pemulihan lahan gambut

Indonesia's Peatland and Mangrove Restoration Agency (BRGM) was established in 2016 after the catastrophic 2015 fires. Its method: blocking drainage canals (pengeringan) to rewet (pembasahan kembali) the peat and raise the water table (meja air) again.

By the time BRGM was dissolved in December 2024, it had restored 1.6 million hectares of peatland and 84,000 hectares of mangrove forests across seven provinces. Restoration work continues under the Ministry of Environment.

13 million hectares of degraded peatland remain. 1.6 million is a significant start — but less than 13% of the total that needs attention.

Communities · hak masyarakat

Legal action and land rights · hak ulayat

Communities are increasingly using Indonesian courts to challenge plantation concessions. The Awyu and Moi peoples of Papua both won significant rulings in 2023–2024, halting concessions on their customary land (tanah adat).

NGOs such as WALHI (Friends of the Earth Indonesia) provide legal support (dukungan hukum) and document land conflicts. International attention on cases like these puts pressure on companies and the government.

Winning in court does not automatically deliver land ownership (kepemilikan tanah). Legal victories are important but rarely the end of the fight.

The honest picture · gambaran jujur

None of these actions has stopped palm oil expansion. Deforestation in 2024 surged 53% compared to the previous year, the worst since 2016. The moratorium is real but enforcement is weak. Certification schemes cover a fraction of the industry. The EUDR is the most powerful external lever — but it only covers the EU market. As long as demand keeps growing from China, India and Indonesia's own biodiesel program, the pressure to clear more land will remain. The question is not whether to have a palm oil industry, but whether the world can agree on what standards it must meet.
